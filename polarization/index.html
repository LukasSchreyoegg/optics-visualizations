How it works

The idea. Light is a transverse wave: its electric field oscillates perpendicular to the beam. In linearly polarised light it always oscillates along one line. A polariser has a transmission axis; it lets through only the part of the field that points along this axis and absorbs the rest.

Malus' law. If the light oscillates at an angle Δ to the axis, only the projection of the field gets through, and the intensity goes with the square of the field:

E′ = E cos Δ   ⇒   I′ = I cos2Δ

Behind the polariser the light oscillates along its axis, so the next polariser measures Δ from there. For a chain of polarisers this gives a product of cos2 terms.

Unpolarised light from the lamp oscillates in all directions at random. The first polariser lets through the average of cos2 over all directions, which is exactly one half:

⟨cos2Δ⟩ = 1/2   ⇒   I1 = I0/2, whatever its angle.

Three polarisers. Two crossed polarisers (Δ = 90°) block everything. Put a third one in between at 45° and light gets through again: I = I0/2 · cos245° · cos245° = I0/8. The middle polariser does not add light; it projects the field onto a new direction, from which the last one can take a part.

How it is computed. The field is a two-dimensional vector, and each polariser projects it onto its axis, E′ = (E · u) u. The check compares these projections with the closed formula of cos2 terms.

Not included. Real polarisers leak a little (finite extinction ratio, Skript 3.2.5) and absorb some light even along their axis; reflections at their surfaces.
