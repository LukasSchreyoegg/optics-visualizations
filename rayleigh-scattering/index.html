The idea. Two things together explain both colours. First, air molecules scatter blue light about six times more strongly than red light (σ ∝ λ−4). The blue that is scattered out of the sunlight reaches you from every direction: that is the blue sky. Second, the lower the sun, the longer its light travels through the air. On the horizon the path is about 35 times longer than with the sun straight overhead, so nearly all of the blue is gone before the light reaches you.

Scattering by one molecule. The light field drives the electrons of a molecule far below their resonance (ω ≪ ω0, resonances in the UV). The induced dipole p = αε0E oscillates with an amplitude almost independent of ω, and a dipole radiates a power proportional to its acceleration squared, P ∝ ω4p02. Instead of guessing ω0, I take the polarisability from the measured refractive index of air, since the same α causes dispersion:

σ(λ) = 24π3 / (λ4 Ns2) · [(n2 − 1)/(n2 + 2)]2 · FK

n(λ): Edlén-type formula for standard air (Peck & Reeder 1972, 15 °C, 1013.25 hPa). Ns = p/(kBT) = 2.55·1025 m−3. FK = 1.049 corrects for the non-spherical N2 and O2 molecules (King factor). The small rise of n towards the blue is the resonance term (ω02 − ω2)−2; it makes the effective exponent slightly steeper than −4.

Why Nσ also near the ground. In an ideal gas the molecules sit independently of each other (Poisson statistics, ⟨ΔN2⟩ = ⟨N⟩). The sideways scattered waves then add in intensity, so the scattering coefficient is β = Nσ at every altitude. In liquids and solids this is not true, because density fluctuations are suppressed.

Atmosphere and path length. Isothermal (T = 288 K), N(z) = Ns e−z/H with scale height H = kBT/(mg) = 8.4 km, cut at 100 km. Spherical Earth, R = 6371 km.

τ(λ) = σ(λ) ∫ N ds + σO3(λ) ∫ nO3 ds,   T = e−τ,   X = ∫N ds / ∫N dz

The path integrals are done numerically (trapezoidal rule with quadratic spacing). The air mass X is not a slider: it follows from the geometry.

Ozone. A layer around 22 km (300 Dobson units, Gaussian profile, 6 km width) absorbs in the Chappuis band, modelled as a Gaussian with a peak cross section of 5·10−21 cm2 at 600 nm and a width of 60 nm (good to about 30 %). At noon it hardly matters (vertical optical depth 0.04 at 600 nm). At twilight the light crosses the ozone layer over hundreds of kilometres; ozone then removes orange light and keeps the sky overhead blue instead of grey (Hulburt 1953).

Sky light. Single scattering along each line of sight. Sunlight reaching a point is attenuated on its way in, the scattered light again on its way to you. Points in the Earth's shadow get no sunlight, which is why the sky goes dark after sunset. Rayleigh phase function p(Θ) = 3(1 + cos2Θ)/(16π).

The picture. Heights are hugely exaggerated (logarithmic height scale). The colour of the beam at a drawn point is computed for the real altitude of that point: it is the light left after crossing all the air above it. The dots next to the beam are the light scattered out of it there (spectrum ∝ E(λ)T(λ)σ(λ)), brighter where the air is denser. The air layer is painted with the sky the person sees: its bottom is the horizon, its top the zenith, and the side of the sun is the view towards the sun.

Sun and colours. The sun is a black body at 5772 K above the atmosphere, and the white balance is set so that this light is white. Colours come from the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (analytic fit by Wyman, Sloan & Shirley 2013), converted to sRGB. The exposure adapts partly to the overall brightness of the sky, like the eye does (square root of the brightness ratio, at most 6×): a sunset does not simply turn black, but the night still does.

Cloud (optional). Water droplets with r = 10 μm are much larger than λ (size parameter 2πr/λ ≈ 80–170). Their extinction efficiency is Qext ≈ 2 for all visible wavelengths, so a cloud does not prefer any colour. Optical depth from liquid water content LWC = 0.3 g/m³ and thickness Δz (base at 1 km):

τc = 3 · LWC · Δz / (2 ρw r),   Td ≈ 1 / (1 + ¾(1 − g)τc),   Rd = 1 − Td

Td is the diffuse transmission of a non-absorbing layer in the two-stream (Eddington) approximation with asymmetry parameter g = 0.85; it is only good to roughly 10–20 %. A thick cloud sends most of the light back up: brilliant white from an aeroplane, grey from below.

Not included. Aerosols (real sunsets are darker and deeper red, the real sky is whiter), multiple scattering in clear air (the computed sky is somewhat too dark overhead), refraction (on the horizon the real sun appears about 0.5° higher), light reflected by the ground.
